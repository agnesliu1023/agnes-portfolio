01

A rain response, not another umbrella

rainNet helps people get an umbrella right when rain starts and return it anywhere nearby — without carrying one all day. It combines a network of rental stations, a kiosk interface, a mobile portal and a brand system into one service.

The case study runs in the order the work was done: why it started, the final service, then the Discover, Define and Develop work behind it.

02

Why I started

I began with a general interest in sustainability and in finding everyday friction in shared urban spaces. Instead of starting with a product, I ran quick field observations at London’s everyday threshold sites — schools, residential entrances, hotels and other dense transition points — to see where small but repeated problems occur.

Weather quickly emerged as the key issue. Rain interrupted movement in ordinary but frustrating ways: people paused at exits, crowded under small awnings and checked their phones. Rain in London is not just weather — it is a recurring source of friction connected to both mobility and waste.

03

Deliver: the final service

A station at the doorway, a screen that rents in seconds, and a network that lets you return anywhere nearby. Four ideas carry the service; the full design boards follow.

Feature 01Rent in seconds
Feature 02Always one ready at the door
Feature 03Sealed, dry and locked
Feature 04A network, not a kiosk
Feature 01 / 04

Rent in seconds

Connect your portal on the screen, pay by card or phone, and the rack turns an umbrella to the door. The screen always shows the weather, with an embedded forecast.

Feature 02 / 04

Always one ready at the door

A rotating rack keeps a dry umbrella at the front while returned ones dry at the back.

Feature 03 / 04

Sealed, dry and locked

The cabin stays closed between rentals. Magnetic locks stop theft, and a dryer in the base blows warm air through umbrellas that were just returned.

Feature 04 / 04

A network, not a kiosk

Stations sit at doorways across a neighbourhood, so you rent at one and return at another. Status LEDs and a rain-probability light read from across the street.

04

Discover

Site observations. I observed campus entrances, residential gates, hotel drop-off points and station-adjacent exits — everyday transition points where small disruptions quickly affect movement and comfort. Rather than starting with a product idea, I looked for repeated moments of friction: where people hesitated, how they used shelter, what they carried, and how the environment shaped their decisions.

AEIOU framework

To structure what I saw, I analyzed activities, environments, interactions, objects and users across 50 observations.

Interviews

Observation showed where friction happened; interviews explained why. I asked people how they prepare for rain, what they do when caught without an umbrella, and why existing options feel inconvenient or unreliable.

Many said they would not carry an umbrella for a short or uncertain trip; others bought cheap umbrellas only when necessary and rarely kept them for long.

Secondary research

Secondary research placed the observed friction in a wider context, rather than treating it as an isolated inconvenience.

05

Define

Key finding. Across every space the same pattern appeared: people were not struggling because rain was unusual, but because protection was inconsistent, badly timed, and inconvenient to carry, share or trust at the point of need.

Insights

Beyond the visible inconvenience, the findings pointed to deeper issues of timing, trust and unequal impact across users — which turned raw research into design direction.

Problem statement

In dense urban London, rain creates repeated friction during short everyday transitions between buildings, entrances and transport-adjacent spaces. Many people don’t prepare for short or uncertain rain, which leads to reactive behaviour — waiting under shelter, rushing, buying cheap umbrellas, or getting wet. Existing options are inconvenient, wasteful or untrustworthy, especially for people carrying bags or luggage, or moving more slowly.

How might we

How might we help people in dense shared urban environments respond to rain more easily, without depending on last-minute, wasteful or unreliable solutions?

06

Develop

Four lenses. Using the insights as a starting point, I explored the problem through access, placement, trust and reuse — to understand how a better rain-response service could work across physical, digital and behavioural touchpoints.

Brainstorming

Brainstorming showed the problem could not be solved by form alone. The strongest ideas combined quick access, threshold placement, visible trust and easy reuse — which shaped the final direction: a station-based shared umbrella system for short urban trips.

Concept comparison

Rather than jumping to the final station, I compared four service models — immediate access, site-based borrowing, distributed sharing and weather-led prompting — testing not only how fast each solved the immediate problem, but how well it supported trust, reuse and everyday movement.

Chosen direction

The networked station model was strongest because it addressed both short-term friction and long-term value: unlike purchase-based or single-site systems, it supports spontaneous access, visible maintenance and multi-location return in one connected service.

User flow and relationships

I reduced the experience to the shortest, clearest path, so people can act quickly once rain becomes a problem — designed around immediate access, visible trust and easy return.

Early sketches and prototypes

Sketches and quick prototypes tested the station’s form, the rack mechanism and the kiosk layout before the final renders.
